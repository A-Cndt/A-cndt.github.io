The Sun: understanding our star

Published on January 22, 2025 by Alexandre Condette

The first article of a series dedicated to the Solar System. And to start, there is no better subject than our star: the Sun. A source of light, heat, and above all, an astrophysical object still full of mysteries.

The Sun: an ordinary star… but essential

The Sun is a G2V spectral type star. In other words, a medium-sized, stable star located on the main sequence. It may seem ordinary on a cosmic scale, but it is absolutely essential for us.

Without it, life on Earth would not exist. It provides the energy required for climate, biological cycles, and photosynthesis.

A giant plasma sphere

The Sun is mainly composed of hydrogen (~75%) and helium (~24%). The rest consists of trace heavier elements.

Its diameter reaches 1.39 million kilometers: about 1.3 million Earths could fit inside it. It accounts for more than 99% of the mass of the Solar System.

Located 150 million kilometers from Earth (1 AU), it sits perfectly within the habitable zone.

How does the Sun produce its energy?

At the core of the Sun, conditions are extreme: over 15 million degrees Celsius. This is where nuclear fusion occurs.

Hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy.

This energy then takes nearly 100,000 years to reach the surface before escaping into space in just 8 minutes to reach Earth.

A layered structure

  • Core: nuclear fusion and energy production
  • Radiative zone: slow energy transport via photons
  • Convective zone: hot and cool plasma movements
  • Photosphere: visible surface of the Sun
  • Chromosphere & corona: highly dynamic outer atmosphere

An active and unstable Sun

Unlike its calm appearance, the Sun is extremely dynamic. Its activity is driven by its magnetic field.

Sunspots

Sunspots are cooler regions caused by magnetic disturbances. They follow an approximately 11-year cycle.

Solar flares

Solar flares release enormous amounts of energy and particles into space. When they reach Earth, they can disrupt satellites, GPS systems, and power grids.

Why the Sun is fundamental

The Sun is the foundation of life on Earth. It powers biological and climate systems.

Photosynthesis depends directly on it, making all life on Earth possible.

It is also a natural laboratory for understanding stellar physics.

Exploring the Sun

Several space missions now allow us to study it closely.

The Parker Solar Probe gets closer than ever before to study the solar corona.

SOHO, a joint NASA/ESA mission, has been observing the Sun since 1995 and has revolutionized our understanding of its activity.

These missions help us better understand solar phenomena and anticipate their impact on our technologies.

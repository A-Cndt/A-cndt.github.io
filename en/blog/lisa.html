Gravitational Waves, ESA’s Bold LISA Mission

Published on January 14, 2025 by Alexandre Condette

For this first article, I wanted to talk about a subject I am deeply passionate about, and on which I had the opportunity to work during my final internship at CPPM: gravitational waves and the LISA space project.

Gravitational waves: what are they?

“Gravitational waves” may sound like something out of science fiction, yet this phenomenon is very real.

They are one of the most recent and fascinating discoveries in modern physics. To understand them, we need to dive into general relativity and our understanding of the Universe.

A concept born with Einstein

The story begins in 1915 with Albert Einstein’s theory of general relativity.

Unlike Newton, who described gravity as a force acting at a distance, Einstein described it as a curvature of spacetime.

Imagine a trampoline: when you place a heavy ball on it, the surface bends. Massive objects similarly curve spacetime around them.

Einstein later showed that when massive objects accelerate, they create ripples in this fabric: gravitational waves.

What is a gravitational wave?

A gravitational wave is a distortion of spacetime that propagates at the speed of light.

These waves are produced by extremely energetic events: black hole collisions, neutron star mergers, supernovae, and early Universe processes.

The effect is extremely small — far smaller than the size of a proton.

Why is it a revolution?

Gravitational waves open a completely new way of observing the Universe.

Instead of detecting light, detectors “listen” directly to spacetime vibrations.

This allows us to study invisible objects like black holes and phenomena inaccessible to traditional telescopes.

How do we detect gravitational waves?

A century-long confirmation

For more than 100 years, gravitational waves remained theoretical.

In 2015, LIGO finally detected the first signal from the merger of two black holes.

This discovery marked the beginning of a new era in astronomy.

LIGO: extreme precision

LIGO uses laser interferometry.

A laser beam is split into two perpendicular arms. A passing gravitational wave slightly changes their length.

The measured variations are incredibly small: about one billionth the size of a proton.

Virgo: the European partner

The Virgo detector in Italy works on the same principle as LIGO.

By combining data from multiple detectors, scientists can locate sources much more precisely.

A promising future

Since 2015, dozens of signals have been detected, revealing black hole and neutron star mergers.

Future projects like the Einstein Telescope and KAGRA will further increase sensitivity.

LISA: detecting gravitational waves from space

Ground-based detectors are limited by seismic noise, vibrations, and the atmosphere.

To overcome these limitations, ESA is developing LISA (Laser Interferometer Space Antenna), a revolutionary space mission.

A space-based detector

Unlike LIGO and Virgo, LISA will operate in space and detect low-frequency gravitational waves.

These correspond mainly to supermassive black hole mergers at the center of galaxies.

Technical concept

LISA will consist of three satellites forming an equilateral triangle with 2.5 million km arms.

Laser beams will measure extremely small distance variations.

Why is LISA a revolution?

  • Supermassive black hole mergers
  • Binary systems in our galaxy
  • Early Universe signatures
  • Unknown physical phenomena

A new window on the cosmos

LISA could also help explore fundamental cosmological mysteries such as dark matter, dark energy, and the first moments after the Big Bang.

The launch is expected in the late 2030s.

With LISA, humanity will gain a new gravitational observatory in space, opening an entirely new vision of the Universe.
